Education, GDP, and Unemployment: A Cross-Country Analysis of the EU

EU-27 Analysis

Back

Focus

Education, GDP, unemployment

Source

Eurostat CSV exports

Scope

27 EU countries, 2015-2024

Tools

Python, pandas, SciPy, seaborn

Description

Motivation

I started this project because I was curious if I can find relationships between education, income, and unemployment in EU data. I wanted to see if the data supports the common assumption that higher education leads to higher income and lower unemployment.

What I did

I used Eurostat data to compare the share of 25-34 year olds with tertiary education, GDP per capita, and unemployment across the 27 EU countries.

I imported the three CSV files into Python, cleaned the column names, filtered the data down to the 27 EU countries, and merged everything by country and year. That gave me 270 complete data points for 2015-2024 with no missing values after the merge.

From there I checked basic summaries and then calculated Pearson correlations country by country to see how strong the relationships were over time.

Tools & skills used

Python, pandas, NumPy, SciPy, matplotlib, seaborn, and linear regression. On the data side I worked with Eurostat CSV exports and a merged country-year table.

Challenges & mistakes

The main limitation is that correlation is not causation. A strong relationship does not prove that education causes higher GDP or lower unemployment. I would also improve the visual story next time by creating more than one clean chart from the final dataset instead of relying on a single plot.

Result & reflection

The pattern was still interesting: Luxembourg was consistently the GDP per capita outlier, Greece had the highest unemployment from 2015 to 2020, and Spain from 2021 to 2024. For me, the biggest takeaway was that the data supports a meaningful relationship, but it also forces you to stay honest about the limits of what the numbers can actually prove.

Quick notes

27 countries, 10 years, 3 indicators. The merged dataset stayed clean, which made the analysis more trustworthy and the later correlation checks easier to read.

One honest takeaway

If I did this again, I would add one or two more targeted charts for the weaker countries too, not just the patterns that look strongest at first glance.
